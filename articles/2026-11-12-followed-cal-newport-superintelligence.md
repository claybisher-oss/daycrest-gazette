*This sample uses a September post, since it's one of the most-discussed pieces Newport has written this fall. Daily runs cover only new posts.*

Cal Newport, a Georgetown computer scientist, wrote two posts in a row this fall about the AI industry. The first was a sharp reaction to several Anthropic employees saying publicly that the kind of AI they build could kill billions of people. This follow-up slows down and states his argument more carefully.

## The argument in three steps

![Newport's argument: the myth drives the race, and the race drives the harm](img/2026-11-12-newport-argument.svg)

1. **Superintelligence is a story, not a plan.** Newport doesn't think today's AI is on a path to becoming an all-knowing digital mind. He treats that idea as closer to a myth: in his telling, some in the industry speak about it almost religiously, as a coming savior or destroyer.
2. **Chasing the story causes real damage anyway.** His example: experiments in which AI "agents" (programs that can take actions on their own, not just answer questions) were given dangerous abilities, had restrictions removed, and were let loose in poorly fenced test environments, all to hit a milestone on a hacking test seen as a step toward superintelligence.
3. **None of that is inevitable.** The harms, he argues, are side effects of recklessness, not of AI itself. If labs focused on useful products and ordinary safety practices, AI could keep improving without the regular waves of dread.

## Where others disagree

The people Newport is responding to would say he underrates how fast these systems are improving, and that warning loudly is the responsible thing to do if the risk is real. Others in the debate agree with him; the critic Gary Marcus called his take close to his own. The real disagreement is less about whether AI can cause harm than about where the harm comes from: the technology itself, or the race to build it.

## Why it matters

How you think about where AI's dangers come from shapes how you use it day to day. Newport's practical point fits ordinary life: judge the tools by what they actually do, not by the story told about them.
