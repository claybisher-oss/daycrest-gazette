Most of Kentucky's insects spend winter as eggs, larvae or sleeping adults. The honey bee does something stranger: the whole colony stays awake and keeps itself warm through the cold months, living off the honey it stored up all summer.

## Know it

A honey bee is a slim, fuzzy bee about half an inch long, with amber-and-brown bands on its abdomen. Bumblebees are bigger and rounder, and they don't make it through winter as a colony: only new queens survive, alone and underground. In November, a bee you see moving around a hive entrance on a warm afternoon is almost certainly a honey bee.

## How the winter cluster works

![The cluster: a warm core, an insulating shell, and bees rotating between them](img/2026-11-12-honey-bee-winter-cluster.svg)

When it gets cold, the colony pulls together into a tight ball on the honeycomb, with the queen near the middle.

- **The core.** Bees in the center make heat by shivering their flight muscles without moving their wings. It works like an idling engine.
- **The shell.** Bees on the outside pack tightly, heads inward, and act as a living blanket. The colder it gets, the tighter they pack.
- **Taking turns.** Bees move between the cold outside and the warm inside, so no one bee freezes on the edge.
- **Fuel.** All that shivering burns honey. Over the winter, the cluster slowly eats its way up through the stored comb.

## Winter bees are different bees

A worker bee born in summer usually lives only a few weeks; she wears herself out foraging. Bees raised in late fall are built differently: they carry extra body fat and protein stores and can live for months, long enough to carry the colony through to spring. The males (drones) don't get that chance; colonies push them out in fall to save food.

## Where to look

On a sunny winter day in the 50s, watch any hive entrance, or a shed eave or hollow tree where a wild colony lives. You may see bees making quick "cleansing flights" (bees won't relieve themselves inside the hive) and hurrying back in. In late winter, the first blooms they visit are often low weeds like henbit and purple deadnettle in lawns and fields.

## Fun fact

Honey bees aren't native to North America. European colonists brought them over in the 1600s, and they spread ahead of the settlers; the bee's arrival was often taken as a sign that settlers weren't far behind.

## Why it matters

A colony that survives the winter is ready to pollinate orchards, gardens and clover the moment spring comes. A long cold snap matters less than whether the colony stored enough honey and went into fall strong. Their winter is a quiet lesson in shared work: each bee taking a turn on the cold edge, so all of them make it to spring.
