After decades tied up at a pier, one of the most famous ships in American history has a new job. On Thursday, Okaloosa County, Florida, sent the SS United States to the bottom of the Gulf about 22 nautical miles southwest of Destin to become the largest artificial reef in the world ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/news/); site details: [Wanderlust](https://www.wanderlustmagazine.com/?p=185448)).

## The ship

![The SS United States beside the Titanic, to scale](img/2026-11-12-ship-lengths.svg)

Entering service in 1952, the *United States* was built to be the fastest and safest liner afloat, and on her first voyage she crossed the Atlantic faster than any liner before or since. That record still stands. She was also bigger than the Titanic, at about 990 feet long, and was secretly designed so the Navy could turn her into a fast troop transport if war came ([Stars and Stripes](https://www.stripes.com/history/2026-10-27/ss-united-states-sinking-date-largest-reef-23279520.html)). Jet travel ended the age of the great liners. She was retired in 1969 and spent decades docked in Philadelphia while preservation groups tried to save her. Okaloosa County bought her in 2024 and spent months in Mobile, Alabama, stripping her for reef use ([USNI News](https://news.usni.org/?p=126505)).

## Why sink a ship on purpose?

A cleaned-out steel hull on the sea floor quickly becomes a home for sea life. Coral, sponges and algae grow on the steel; small fish shelter in the structure; bigger fish follow. Coastal communities sink old ships to create fishing and diving spots and to take pressure off natural reefs. Before sinking, the ship is stripped of fuel, oils and other pollutants.

She now replaces the aircraft carrier USS Oriskany, sunk off Pensacola in 2006, as the world's largest artificial reef. Researchers plan to monitor how sea life settles on her.

## Why it matters

It's a bittersweet ending. A ship that couldn't be saved as a museum will still be visited, by divers instead of tourists, and it gives the ocean a new reef.
