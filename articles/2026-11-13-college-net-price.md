College prices are famously hard to read. A report from NPR and The Hechinger Report looks at one fix colleges are trying: a simple income line below which tuition is free.

## The promises

- **Middlebury College** in Vermont now covers tuition for families earning up to $175,000.
- **Princeton, the University of Chicago, MIT, Harvard, Swarthmore, Penn and Davidson** have similar pledges, with cutoffs between about $175,000 and $250,000.
- Smaller versions exist at Earlham, Wake Forest, Marist and New Hampshire's public colleges.

## The fine print

"Free tuition" isn't "free college." At Middlebury, housing, food and other costs add about $24,000 a year on top of a listed total price near $97,000. Middlebury itself says the new promise changes the bill for only a few dozen extra families per class, because most families in that income range were already paying little.

## The real problem: the listed price

A Hechinger review of federal data found 445 colleges where no one pays the advertised price, and at private nonprofit four-year schools fewer than one family in five pays it. Yet surveys show nearly 80% of families have crossed a school off their list because of price, and about 70% find financial aid confusing. One economist quoted in the story argues the problem is less that college is unaffordable for these families than that prices aren't stated clearly.

## What to do with this

Every college must post a **net price calculator** on its website. Plugging in your own numbers gives a far better estimate than the sticker price or a headline promise.

## Why it matters

Families may be ruling out schools they could actually afford. Clear prices, more than new promises, would help the most.
