Austin Kleon, author of *Steal Like an Artist*, often blogs a single afternoon's chain of curiosity. This post follows one thread from a 1980s memoir to today's AI chatbots.

## The trail

1. **Crichton in therapy.** A friend pointed Kleon to the psychiatry chapter of Michael Crichton's 1988 memoir *Travels*. Around 1971, after a separation, Crichton grudgingly began seeing a psychiatrist, tracking whether each session was worth its fee, until a realization in the fifth session that he was insecure. He later ignored his doctor's warnings about his relationships, which turned out to be right.
2. **Crichton on ELIZA.** Kleon then found a 1970 Dick Cavett clip in which Crichton describes ELIZA, a 1960s MIT program by Joseph Weizenbaum that played therapist by reflecting a user's words back as questions. Crichton found it unnervingly effective.
3. **Weizenbaum's alarm.** ELIZA's creator was disturbed by how seriously people took it, and he became one of artificial intelligence's earliest critics. His book *Computer Power and Human Reason* warned against handing human judgment to machines.
4. **The Eliza effect.** Finally, Kleon cites MIT's Sherry Turkle, who tells of Weizenbaum's own staff asking for privacy with the program. Turkle's "Eliza effect" names our habit of crediting machines with understanding because we want so badly to be understood.

## The unspoken contrast

Kleon doesn't spell out a moral, but the post sets two things side by side: a real outside observer (Crichton's doctor) who could see his blind spots, and a machine that can only mirror us back to ourselves.

## Why it matters

As chatbots become everyday companions, the sixty-year-old ELIZA story is worth knowing. Feeling understood and being understood are not the same thing.

## Worth watching?

Yes: it's short and enjoyable, and it models a good reading habit, following one link of curiosity to the next. Light on argument, rich in trails to follow.
