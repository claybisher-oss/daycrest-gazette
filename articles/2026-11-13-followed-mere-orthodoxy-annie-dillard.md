Annie Dillard is best known for *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek*, her Pulitzer-winning account of a year of fierce attention to a Virginia creek. In a reflective essay for Mere Orthodoxy, Jody Benson argues that Dillard's true subject wasn't wild nature at all.

## Where it starts

Benson opens under a maple, watching a squirrel with her toddler and feeling restless, wanting to be somewhere more significant. She names that restlessness a kind of spiritual blindness: unease at being a limited creature who longs for her Maker.

## Dillard, book by book

- ***Holy the Firm*** pictures human beings as strangers in a world they didn't make. Benson suggests Dillard's wilderness is as much inside us as outside.
- ***Living by Fiction*** traces literature's drift from writing about the cosmos, to writing about society, to writing mostly about its own forms.
- ***The Writing Life*** presents writing as a confrontation with one's own limits; Benson connects this to Rilke and to Edith Stein's distinctions between what passes, what lasts, and what is eternal.
- ***Pilgrim at Tinker Creek***, with its turn to the book of Job, she reads as a strong answer to suffering through beauty. She finds Dillard's later *For the Time Being* less satisfying, and sets it beside Jesus' answer about the man born blind in John 9.

Benson also notes, with some sympathy, that Dillard turned to dramatic nature writing partly out of fear that her domestic life would bore readers.

## Her conclusion

We tend to think spiritual growth requires an expedition. Benson argues the frontier is closer: attention to what's in front of us, and willingness to accept our particular calling in ordinary, embodied days.

## Why it matters

It's a thoughtful corrective for anyone who reads nature writing or keeps a journal: the extraordinary is mostly found by paying attention to the ordinary.

## Worth watching?

Yes, if you read slowly and like literary essays. It's dense in the middle but rewarding; have a copy of *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek* nearby.
