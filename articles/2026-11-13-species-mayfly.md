Most insects are long gone by mid-November, but turn over a rock in any clean Central Kentucky creek and you'll find mayfly nymphs clinging underneath. On gray, drizzly afternoons like today's, some of them are still hatching.

## Know it

- **Shape:** a slender body that curves up at the tail end, with two or three long, hair-like tails.
- **Wings:** held straight up over the back, pressed together like a sail. Caddisflies fold their wings like a tent; stoneflies lay theirs flat.
- **This time of year:** the common fall species are tiny "blue-winged olives" (genus *Baetis*), about the size of a grain of rice, with smoky gray wings and an olive body.

## The life cycle

1. **Nymph** (most of the year): lives under creek rocks, breathing through feathery gills along its sides and grazing on algae.
2. **Dun** (hours to a day): swims to the surface, splits its skin and floats off as a dull-winged adult, drifting like a tiny sailboat while its wings dry.
3. **Spinner** (a day or two): molts once more into a shiny adult, mates over the water and dies. Adults have no working mouthparts.

## Where to look locally

Riffles of the small limestone creeks that feed the Kentucky River; the Dix River below Herrington Dam; and the Cumberland River tailwater, where trout key on blue-winged olives all winter. Watch slow water on a cloudy afternoon for small upright "sails" drifting along, and for gentle dimples where fish sip them.

## Fun fact

Mayflies are the only insects that molt again **after** they already have functioning wings. That dun-to-spinner molt is unique in the insect world.

## Why it matters

Mayfly nymphs need clean, well-oxygenated water, so biologists use them as a living water-quality test: a creek full of mayflies is usually a healthy creek. They're also a major food for trout, bass, sunfish and songbirds.
