In Monday's essay, Doug Wilson makes a deliberately one-sided case aimed at two audiences at once. To free-market classical liberals, he says their project will not survive unless it openly acknowledges Christ. To fellow Christian nationalists drifting toward government-heavy economics, he says they need the economic wisdom of classical liberalism or they will end up on the fringe and broke.

## Wilson's argument, at its strongest

He begins with a critique both sides of politics may recognize: the habit of treating the state as an endless source of benefits, with factions fighting mainly over who controls the spigot. Against this he sets Adam Smith's picture of the market, in which many people pursuing their own interests produce widespread benefits nobody planned. His homely illustration: open competition gives a town several gas stations full of fuel, while central planning gives one efficient station with nothing to sell.

The heart of the essay is the claim that markets cannot manufacture the people they depend on. Prosperity tends to breed complacency and envy, which in turn invite socialism. Free markets need people capable of disciplined, long-term self-interest rather than self-indulgence, and Wilson argues that kind of character is formed by the gospel. In his ordering, free people make free markets, not the reverse. He cites Deuteronomy 32:15 on Israel growing fat and forgetting God, along with a Cotton Mather line about prosperity devouring the faithfulness that produced it.

From there he concludes that classical liberalism must become "classical Christian liberalism" to remain itself. He blames today's troubles less on markets than on cozy state-business partnerships, and he warns reactionaries that strongman economics will not save anything. Real reform, he says, needs wise leadership and a virtuous people, which depends on preaching. Finally, he reads the invisible hand as the hand of a personal God, so that trusting unplanned outcomes is itself an act of faith. He offers "Christian federalism" as an alternative label for those uneasy with "Christian nationalism."

## Where traditions differ

Wilson's point that markets rely on moral capital they cannot replenish is widely shared, echoed by Catholic social teaching, by conservative thinkers, and by many secular economists. Disagreement comes elsewhere. Catholic and many mainline traditions affirm markets but give more weight to the common good, labor, and a supportive role for the state than Wilson's minimal-state view allows. Christians in the Anabaptist and Baptist religious-liberty traditions question tying the gospel to any political or economic program, worrying that "Christian" becomes a label for a platform. And many scholars of Smith read the invisible hand as a modest metaphor rather than a theological claim.

## Why it matters

The essay is a clear window into an intra-conservative debate over economics, and it poses a fair question to any reader: what forms the virtues a free society needs, and who is responsible for forming them?
