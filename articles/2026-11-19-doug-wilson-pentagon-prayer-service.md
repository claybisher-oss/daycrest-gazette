Doug Wilson, the Idaho pastor and author, has posted the text of a prayer and short address he delivered at a prayer service held at the Pentagon. After a prayer of thanks for salvation and Scripture, he organizes his remarks around two questions, where we are and where we are going, and answers with seven theological bearings.

## Wilson's case, in its strongest form

**Creator and creature.** God alone is necessary and transcendent; everything else depends on him. Because God's will is not subject to courts, legislatures, or elections, moral standards come from outside the world rather than from consensus.

**The Incarnation.** The Son took on flesh in order to die. Bethlehem pointed to Calvary, and Wilson insists the nation has no deep hope apart from that gospel.

**Shared guilt.** Everyone, including Christians who think they are on the right side, is implicated in sin. Polarization tempts each faction to see villains only across the aisle. This is perhaps the most striking note given the audience: a call to humility from people who work inside flawed institutions.

**By what standard?** When Christians are called evil by people who deny objective evil exists, Wilson says they should ask what standard is being applied and why it binds everyone.

**Not whether, but which.** Every society legislates morality and has some final court of appeal, a functional god, whether that is Allah, the triune God, the people, or media opinion.

**God's shrewdness and the resurrection pattern.** Drawing on Herbert Schlossberg and G.K. Chesterton, he argues that God repeatedly brings triumph out of apparent disaster, the cross being the supreme case, and that the church has seemed to die many times only to rise again. He closes on the old proverb that "God draws straight with crooked lines."

## Where traditions differ

Much here is common Christian ground: divine transcendence, the Incarnation, universal sinfulness, and resurrection hope are confessed across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant churches. The disagreement lies mostly in application. Wilson's "not whether, but which" framing reflects his broader postmillennial and Christian-nationalist project, which expects public law to acknowledge Christ explicitly. Many Baptists, Anabaptists, and other Christians committed to religious liberty would accept that every society carries moral assumptions yet still argue that a pluralist state should not formally establish any faith, and that the church's witness is better kept distinct from state power. Others would ask how a prayer service inside a military headquarters should balance prophetic distance with pastoral presence.

## Why it matters

As a compact statement of a Reformed public theology, the address is clear and memorable, and its warning against self-righteous partisanship applies well beyond Wilson's own circles.

## Worth reading in full?

Yes, it is brief, and the seven points are easy to weigh one at a time.
