Miles Smith, a historian of the American South, opens his Mere Orthodoxy essay with a memory of a conservative Presbyterian church in North Carolina where a pastor's wife campaigned against gambling and the state lottery. For Smith that scene captures what evangelical politics used to be: an effort to restrain vice so that society would be better prepared to hear the gospel.

## Never apolitical

Smith pushes back on the common story that evangelicals were once private, individualistic believers who only entered politics in the 1970s. Drawing on historians such as Richard Carwardine, Anne Loveland, Frances FitzGerald, and George Marsden, he describes a long tradition of moral reform, from temperance onward, in both North and South. Even after the fundamentalist-modernist split, evangelicals kept leading on questions of vice, and they expected the people advancing those causes to be personally upright.

## The turn

Smith's thesis is that this tradition broke down with evangelical support for Donald Trump. In his account, evangelical leaders through the Obama years still acted as an independent bloc with recognizable priorities. After 2016 they traded that independence for access to the presidency. He argues this left evangelical moral claims not only weaker but incoherent, since a movement historically concerned with gambling now follows a former casino owner.

He anticipates the reply that the movement has delivered on immigration, the border, and trade. His answer is that these were never distinctive evangelical causes and that evangelicals are not the ones leading on them. As a sign of decline he cites a recent state ballot fight over sports gambling in which evangelicals were not the driving force. He also notes Thomas Kidd's observation that "evangelical" increasingly names a political identity more than a pattern of church life.

## Where it leaves things

Smith concludes that evangelicals remain, in his words, "only a voting bloc, rather than policy initiators." He suggests there is still room to lead on issues like gambling and predatory lending, but thinks that is hard while the current president leads the party.

## Why it matters

Whatever one's politics, the essay raises a question worth taking seriously: what does it mean for a church to have a public witness of its own rather than borrowing one from a party? Readers will weigh Smith's verdict on 2016 differently, but his historical sketch of evangelical reform movements is a useful reminder that concern for public virtue has deep roots.
