In Wednesday's Wake-Up Call, J.D. Walt continues a theme he has been building all week: if relationships are the mission, then the health of a church's friendships is not a side issue to revival but the track it runs on. The reading is Romans 15:1–4, where Paul tells the strong to bear with the weak and to please their neighbors for their good.

## Paul the pastor

Walt reminds readers that Paul's letters, for all their theological weight, were written to build up actual congregations. Much of that work meant tending relationships that had come apart. He points to Philippians 4:2–3, where Paul publicly urges two women, Euodia and Syntyche, to agree in the Lord, and asks a co-worker to help them. Walt pauses to imagine being remembered in Scripture chiefly for a quarrel, then makes the serious point: a dispute that was probably small in itself threatened the work of a young church. In his phrase, "a broken friendship can derail the whole train."

He extends the same reading to Romans itself, suggesting that Paul wrote the letter largely to repair relationships that were endangering the gospel's progress in Rome, and he pairs it with Ephesians 4:3 and its call to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace.

## How the damage starts

The most practical part of the entry is its list of small beginnings: a slight nobody mentions, a hurt kept quiet, a sideways jab, a wound left to fester. None of these looks dangerous on its own. Left alone, they become the unmended relationships that quietly sap a family or congregation.

## A hopeful challenge

Walt frames this not as a rebuke but as an opening. If broken relationships can block renewal, then repairing them might be where early signs of awakening appear. He admits the idea is simple without being easy. The entry closes with a prayer confessing that believers often treat the body of Christ as a mere metaphor, journal prompts inviting readers to name relationships that need repair, and a suggested hymn, "Blest Be the Tie That Binds."

## Why it matters

For anyone keeping a daily rhythm of Scripture and prayer, this is a useful corrective: devotion that never reaches the person across the table is incomplete. Walt's reading of Paul also models something worth practicing in a Bible-in-a-year plan, which is noticing how much of the New Testament is about real people who were not getting along.

## Worth reading in full?

Yes. It is short, and the journal prompts work well as an evening-page exercise.
