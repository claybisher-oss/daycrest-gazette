Hadden Turner reviews *Strip Mine Jesus: Hope for the Rural Church, Small Towns & Overlooked Places* by Charles Cotherman.

## The book's plea
Turner starts with an English village bypassed by a motorway and widens out to rural decline everywhere, borrowing Wendell Berry's idea of forces that pull people and wealth toward the center. Cotherman grew up beside an abandoned strip mine in Pennsylvania, left, and later moved back with his wife to plant a church in Oil City — which, Turner says, earns him a hearing. His core ask: commit to a place for the long haul, or move toward an overlooked one and work alongside the people already there.

## Idols and virtues
Cotherman names three idols that pull Christians away from small places: the celebrity mindset, over-efficiency and autonomy. Against them he sets patience, commitment, long-suffering, tradition and an agrarian outlook. Attention is the keystone — you can't love a place you haven't first paid attention to. The book's hope is theological: God already loves these places and is at work in them, so the church joins in rather than rescues.

## The critique
Turner's main complaint is that the book says too little about shared, communal action — a town needs a common vision built through compromise, not just faithful individuals. He still calls it excellent.

## Why it matters
For anyone in Kentucky's small towns and farm country, the question is close to home: what it means to belong to a rural place, not just live there.
